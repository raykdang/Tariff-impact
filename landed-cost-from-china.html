Landed cost from China to the US: every line, with a worked example

Landed cost is what one unit really costs you once it is in your warehouse: the supplier’s price plus freight, insurance, duty, customs fees, the broker, the bond and the last delivery. Below, each line is explained and one shipment of 2,000 cotton T-shirts from China is worked through, from $3.20 a shirt at the factory to $5.05 a shirt on your shelf.

The example shipment

You import 2,000 cotton T-shirts (tariff code 6109.10.00) from a factory in China at $3.20 a shirt, $6,400 in all, on FOB terms: the supplier delivers to the ship and you pay everything after that. You sell them to shops at $9.00.

The duty rate is real and checked; freight, insurance, broker, bond and delivery are example figures for a small sea shipment. Get your own quotes: they swing with the season, the port and the size of the load.

What it does to your margin at $9.00: without the duty the shirt would land at $3.88 and earn $5.12 (56.9%). With it, it lands at $5.05 and earns $3.95 (43.9%).

Each line, in plain words

Goods

The price on the supplier’s invoice. This is normally the customs value that duty is charged on. If the supplier quotes CIF or DDP, freight (and on DDP the duty) is inside the price: ask for the goods price on its own line. See who pays the tariff under DDP, DAP and FOB.

Freight and insurance

Sea freight is priced by container or, for a part load, by volume. Air is many times dearer and only pays for light, urgent or valuable goods. Cargo insurance is usually a small percentage of the goods’ value. For goods entering the United States, international freight and insurance are normally not part of the value that duty is charged on.

Duty

The rate for your product’s code times the customs value. For goods made in China it is usually three layers that add up: the ordinary duty in the tariff schedule, any older Section 301 China duty for the list your code is on (+25% on lists 1 to 3, +7.5% on list 4A), and the 12.5% country-wide duty on products of China in force since 24 July 2026. Some goods skip the 12.5%: goods under Section 232 such as steel, aluminum, cars and parts, and upholstered wooden furniture, which pay their own duty instead, and goods on the exempt lists. Look your code up with our step-by-step guide.

Examples checked on 25 November 2026: cotton T-shirts 36.5% (clothing from China), toys 12.5% (toys from China).

Merchandise Processing Fee

Customs’ own fee on each formal entry: 0.3464% of the goods’ value, at least $34.58 and at most $670.86, from 1 October 2026. On a small shipment the minimum applies; on our $6,400 it is $34.58, not $22.17. The MPF calculator works it out for any value.

Harbor Maintenance Fee

Goods that arrive by sea also pay a Harbor Maintenance Fee, a small percentage of the value. We have not checked its current rate for this page, so it is left out of the example: your broker will show it on the entry.

Broker and bond

A licensed customs broker files the entry for you and charges per entry. Every formal entry also needs a customs bond: a single-entry bond for one shipment, or a yearly bond if you import often. Both are quotes, not set fees.

Delivery from the port

Trucking or rail from the port to your door, plus any warehouse handling. For Amazon sellers this line is the trip to the fulfilment centre; see the FBA tariff calculator.

The costs people forget

  • The minimum fee. On entries under about $9,983 the Merchandise Processing Fee is a flat $34.58, so it weighs more on small orders.
  • Per-entry costs on small shipments. Broker, bond and fee are close to the same for $6,400 as for $16,000. Here they add $0.13 a shirt; on 5,000 shirts they would add about half that.
  • Customs exams and port storage. If a container is picked for inspection or held at the port, you pay the exam and the storage days.
  • Payment and currency fees. Bank transfers and exchange margins on the supplier payment.
  • Samples, inspections and testing. A pre-shipment inspection, or product safety testing for toys and children’s goods.
  • Anti-dumping or countervailing duty. Some products from China pay one on top of everything above. We did not check any for this page; a broker can tell you whether yours does.

Checklist for your own shipment

  1. Get the supplier’s goods price on its own, and the Incoterm (FOB, CIF, DDP).
  2. Find the tariff code and add up the layers: ordinary duty, any China list, the 12.5% (or Section 232 instead).
  3. Get a freight quote to your port and a delivery quote to your door.
  4. Ask a broker for the entry fee and the bond, and whether any anti-dumping duty applies.
  5. Work out the fee: 0.3464% of the goods’ value, between $34.58 and $670.86.
  6. Add it all and divide by units. Then put the per-unit figures in the calculator to see the margin and the price you need.

Questions

How do I calculate landed cost from China?

Add every cost of getting the goods to your door, then divide by the number of units: the supplier price, freight, cargo insurance, the duty (the rate times the customs value of the goods), the Merchandise Processing Fee, your customs broker, your customs bond and the delivery from the port. On our 2,000-shirt example that is $10,090.58, or $5.05 a shirt.

What is the duty on goods from China today?

It depends on the product’s tariff code. Most goods from China pay three layers: the ordinary duty for the code, any older Section 301 China duty for the list the code is on, and the 12.5% country-wide duty in force since 24 July 2026. A cotton T-shirt pays 16.5% + 7.5% + 12.5% = 36.5%. Checked 25 November 2026.

Is duty charged on the freight as well as the goods?

For goods entering the United States, duty is normally charged on the price paid to the supplier for the goods, not on the international freight and insurance. Your broker states the customs value on the entry. On a DDP or CIF quote, ask the supplier to show the goods price on its own.

Which costs do people forget in landed cost?

The minimum customs fee on small entries, the broker and bond, delivery from the port, customs exams and storage if a container is held, payment and currency fees, and the Harbor Maintenance Fee on sea freight. Each is small on its own, but on a small shipment they can add more than a tenth to the unit cost.
