Should you absorb a tariff or raise prices?

It depends mostly on one thing you can estimate: how many sales you lose when your price goes up. If customers barely react, passing the tariff on keeps more profit. If they react strongly, absorbing it can cost you less. This page shows the sums on one product, then how to run them on yours.

One product, three choices

Take a product you sell for $40. The goods cost $16 to import, shipping is $2 a unit, and other costs that rise with each sale (packaging, card fees) are $4. You sell 500 a month. Before any tariff you make $18 a unit, or $9,000 a month.

Now a tariff of 25% lands on the $16 import cost. That is $4 a unit. The 25% is an example to show the sums, not today's rate for any product.

"Barely react" means a price elasticity of −0.5: a 10% price rise loses about 4.7% of unit sales. "Moderately" is −1.5, where the same rise loses about 13.3%. "Strongly" is −3, where it loses about 24.9%. These are the figures the site's calculator gives for this example, and each one links to the calculator with that case filled in. The calculator shows monthly profit as a change from the $9,000 before the tariff: −$1,565, for instance, is $7,435.

Read across the rows and the answer flips. Where customers barely react, passing the whole tariff on keeps $1,581 a month more than absorbing it. Where they react strongly, passing it on is the worst choice, and absorbing is the best of three bad ones.

Nothing in the table gets you back to $9,000. A tariff is a real cost. The decision is how to lose the least.

Price sensitivity, in plain words

Economists call it price elasticity of demand. It is one number: roughly, the percentage change in units sold for each 1% change in price. If a small price rise of 2% loses you 3% of sales, your number is about −1.5.

A rough rule for a small price rise: raising the price pays as long as that number, ignoring the minus sign, is smaller than your price divided by your profit per unit after the tariff. In the example, that is $40 ÷ $14, about 2.9. Below that, a small rise adds profit. Above it, a rise costs more in lost sales than it brings in. The larger the rise, the lower that limit falls, which is why the calculator is worth five minutes.

Customers tend to react less when:

  • your competitors import the same goods and face the same tariff;
  • the product is a small part of what the customer spends, or hard to replace;
  • you sell on service, speed or trust more than on price.

They react more when a rival sources somewhere with a lower tariff, when the product is easy to compare online, or when the purchase can wait.

How to guess your own number without a study

  1. Look at your last price change. Divide the percentage change in units sold by the percentage change in price. You raised a price 8% and units fell 6%: 6 ÷ 8 gives about −0.75. Compare like with like: the same months a year apart, not December against January.
  2. Look at a sale. A 10% discount that lifted units 20% suggests roughly −2. Discounts overstate it a little, because some buyers only stock up.
  3. Test on a few products. Raise the price on a handful of lines, leave similar ones alone, and compare after a month.
  4. If you have nothing, bracket it. Run the sums at −0.5, −1.5 and −3, as in the table above. If the same choice wins at all three, you have your answer. If it flips, test before you reprice everything.

The division is a rough guide, and it is closest for small price changes.

How much to raise, if you raise

Two targets give two different prices, and people often mix them up.

  • Keep the same dollars per unit. Add the tariff per unit to the price. In the example: $40 + $4 = $44. You still make $18 a unit.
  • Keep the same percentage margin. Divide the new unit cost by one minus your margin. The margin was $18 ÷ $40 = 45%. The new cost is $26. $26 ÷ 0.55 = $47.27.

The second price is $3.27 higher than the first. It protects your percentage, which matters if a lender or a marketplace fee is tied to it, but it asks the customer for more than the tariff cost you. Either way, the rise as a share of the price is smaller than the tariff rate: $4 on $40 is 10%, not 25%. The tariff surcharge calculator turns your own figures into that percentage.

Other moves, before or beside a price rise

  • Split by product. Pass the tariff on where customers react least, and absorb it on the lines that bring people in.
  • Show it as a surcharge. A separate line is easier to remove if the tariff falls. See surcharge or price rise.
  • Ask the supplier to share it. Suppliers who want to keep the order sometimes take part of the cost.
  • Change where you buy. A different origin can carry a different tariff, but the gap has to pay for the cost of moving. See China vs Vietnam vs Mexico vs India: landed cost compared.
  • Cut another cost. Freight, packaging and order size are sometimes easier to move than the price.

Questions

Should I absorb a tariff or raise my prices?

It depends on how many sales you lose when the price goes up. If customers barely react to price, passing the tariff on protects more profit. If they react strongly, absorbing some or all of it can leave you better off. Work out profit under each choice with your own price, cost and volume.

How much should I raise prices because of a tariff?

To keep the same profit per unit in dollars, raise the price by the tariff you pay per unit. To keep the same percentage margin, divide your new unit cost by one minus your margin; that gives a higher price. Then check how many sales the higher price is likely to cost you.

Does a 25% tariff mean a 25% price rise?

No. The tariff applies to the imported cost of the goods, not to the selling price. On an item that costs $16 to import and sells for $40, a 25% tariff is $4, which is 10% of the price.

How the table was worked out

The table uses the same rule as the site's calculator. Units sold after a price change = 500 × (new price ÷ old price) raised to the power of the elasticity, with elasticity set to −0.5, −1.5 and −3. Monthly profit = units × (price − $26 unit cost), rounded to the dollar. No fixed costs are included, since they are the same under every choice. This page quotes no official tariff rate; every rate on it is an example.
