Break-even price after a tariff

There are three break-even prices, not one: the price that keeps your dollars per unit, the price that keeps your percentage margin, and the price that keeps your monthly profit once some customers buy less. This page works all three on one example and gives a table for common tariff rates.

The example

You sell a product for $50. The goods cost $20 to import, shipping is $2 a unit, and other costs that rise with each sale are $3. Your unit cost is $25 and you make $25 a unit, a 50% margin. You sell 400 a month: $10,000 profit.

A tariff of 25% lands on the $20 import value. That is $5 a unit, and your unit cost becomes $30. The 25% is an example to show the sums, not today's rate for any product. For real rates, see US tariff rates by country and how to find your HTS code.

Break-even 1: keep the same dollars per unit

New price = old price + tariff per unit. $50 + $5 = $55. You still make $25 a unit. This is the smallest rise that leaves each sale as profitable in dollars as before, and the easiest to explain to a customer: the price went up by what the tariff costs.

The tariff per unit is the import value times the tariff rate: $20 × 25% = $5. Not the selling price times the rate: that would be $12.50, and far too much.

Break-even 2: keep the same percentage margin

New price = old price × new unit cost ÷ old unit cost. $50 × $30 ÷ $25 = $60. You make $30 a unit, still 50% of the price.

The same answer comes from the textbook form: new unit cost ÷ (1 − margin) = $30 ÷ 0.5 = $60. It asks the customer for $10, twice what the tariff cost you. Use it when something you pay is tied to the percentage, like a marketplace or lender's terms, or when you price every product by a fixed markup. Otherwise it is a price rise on top of the tariff, and customers may see it that way.

Break-even 3: keep the same monthly profit

Both prices above assume you still sell 400 a month. At a higher price you usually sell fewer. The calculator's "Break-even price" tile finds the price that brings monthly profit back to where it was before the tariff, after the lost sales. It needs one more number, your price elasticity: roughly, the percentage of unit sales you lose for each 1% rise in price.

Each figure links to the calculator with that case filled in. "None" is what the calculator shows when no price can do it. With moderate reactions, the best that price alone can earn is $9,938 a month, at $90. With strong reactions, raising the price only makes it worse: the best is $8,230, at $45, which is a cut. In both cases price cannot carry the whole tariff, and the gap has to come from the supplier, other costs, or your profit. Absorb a tariff or raise prices? shows how to estimate your own elasticity.

Break-even prices for common tariff rates

Same example: $50 price, $20 import value, $25 unit cost before the tariff. Rates are examples, not the rate on any product.

10%$2.00$52.00$54.00+4%

50%$10.00$60.00$70.00+20%

The last column is the point most people miss: a 25% tariff on a product whose import value is 40% of its price needs a 10% price rise, not 25%. The smaller the import value is as a share of your price, the smaller the rise.

Work out yours in four steps

  1. Find your import value per unit. The customs value on your entry papers divided by units, usually the price you paid the supplier. Duty is charged on this, not on what you sell for.
  2. Find your total rate. The ordinary duty for your product's code plus any added tariff for its origin. See how to find your HTS code and rate.
  3. Multiply. Import value × rate = tariff per unit. Add it to your price for break-even 1; multiply your price by new cost ÷ old cost for break-even 2.
  4. Check volume. Put your numbers in the calculator and read the "Break-even price" tile at two or three elasticities.

Questions

How do I work out a break-even price after a tariff?

Add the tariff you pay per unit to your old price if you want to keep the same profit per unit in dollars. If you want to keep the same percentage margin, multiply your old price by your new unit cost and divide by your old unit cost. Both assume you sell as many units as before.

Is the break-even price the old price plus the tariff?

Only if you want the same dollars per unit and your sales do not fall. A tariff of $5 a unit on a $50 product gives $55. Keeping the same percentage margin needs more, and if customers buy less at the higher price, even $55 may not keep your monthly profit.

What if no price gets my profit back?

That happens when customers react strongly to price: each rise loses more in sales than it gains per unit. Then price alone cannot recover the tariff, and you need a cheaper supplier, lower costs elsewhere, or to accept a smaller profit. The calculator says when this is the case.

How the figures were worked out

Break-even 1 and 2 are plain arithmetic. Break-even 3 and the units and profit in its table come from the site's calculator: units sold = 400 × (new price ÷ $50) raised to the power of the elasticity, and monthly profit = units × (price − $30), with no fixed costs. This page quotes no official tariff rate; every rate on it is an example.
