A 25% tariff doesn’t mean a 25% price rise: the margin maths

A tariff is charged on what the importer paid for the goods, not on the price in the shop. On a product that sells for four times its import cost, a 25% tariff is a 6.25% price rise if it is passed on dollar for dollar. It becomes a full 25% only when every business along the way adds its usual markup on top of the tariff.

The rule in one line

Price rise = tariff rate × the import value’s share of the price. A 25% tariff on goods that are a quarter of the price is a 6.25% rise; on goods that are half the price, 12.5%; only when the import value is the whole price is it 25%. This holds when the tariff is passed on dollar for dollar, the way most small sellers do it.

The tariff is charged on the customs value: what the importer paid the foreign supplier for the goods. Freight, warehousing, wages, rent, the importer’s margin and the shop’s margin come later, and none of them pays the tariff.

One product, three prices

The same item costs $20 to import. A 25% tariff is $5. Three sellers sell it at three different prices and each passes on exactly $5.

Selling priceImport value as share of priceNew pricePrice rise
$25 (thin margin, e.g. a wholesaler)80%$30+20%
$40 (double the import cost)50%$45+12.5%
$80 (four times, a typical shop price)25%$85+6.25%

When a 25% tariff does become a 25% price rise

Follow the item along the chain: the importer pays $20 and sells to a shop at $40; the shop sells at $80. Now add the $5 tariff, and see what each business does with it.

What each seller doesImporter sells atShop sells atShelf rise
No tariff$40$80—
Both pass on the $5 in dollars$45$85+6.25%
Importer keeps its 50% margin; shop passes on dollars$50$90+12.5%
Both keep their 50% margins$50$100+25%

When every seller doubles its cost, the $5 tariff becomes $20 on the shelf: a 25% rise, and each seller earns more in dollars than before ($25 instead of $20 for the importer, $50 instead of $40 for the shop). So the answer to “does a 25% tariff raise prices 25%?” is: only if everyone in the chain keeps their percentage markup on the tariff too.

If you keep your percentage markup instead, the rise is the tariff rate times the import value’s share of your unit cost (not of your price), which is a bigger share and so a bigger rise.

What it does to the margin

Take a business that imports the item at $20 and sells it at $40: $20 profit a unit, a 50% margin. The 25% tariff adds $5 to its cost.

  • Price held at $40: cost $25, profit $15 instead of $20, margin 37.5%. The tariff took a quarter of the profit per unit.
  • Price $45 (tariff passed on in dollars): profit $20, margin 44.4%. Same dollars, a lower percentage, and a few customers may stop buying.
  • Price $50 (margin kept): profit $25, margin 50%. The customer pays $10 for a $5 tariff.

Which to choose depends on how your customers react to price: see absorb a tariff or raise prices? and break-even price after a tariff.

Questions

Does a 25% tariff raise prices by 25%?

Usually not. The tariff is 25% of the import value, which is only part of the shelf price. If an $80 product cost $20 to import, the tariff is $5, and passing on exactly that is a 6.25% rise. Prices rise by the full 25% only if every seller in the chain keeps the same percentage markup on its higher cost.

How do tariffs affect profit margins?

If the seller keeps its price, the tariff comes straight out of profit per unit: a $5 tariff on a product earning $25 a unit cuts that profit by a fifth. If the seller raises the price by the tariff in dollars, profit per unit stays the same but the percentage margin falls a little, and some customers may buy less.

Is a tariff charged on the retail price?

No. For goods entering the United States, duty is charged on the customs value, normally the price the importer paid the foreign supplier for the goods. Freight, the importer’s markup, the retailer’s markup and sales tax all come later and are not part of it.

Why do some prices rise by more than the tariff?

Because businesses that price by a fixed markup apply it to their new, higher cost. If an importer doubles its cost and a retailer doubles again, a $5 tariff becomes $20 on the shelf. Some sellers also raise prices on goods that pay no tariff, because competitors’ prices went up.

How the figures were worked out

Plain arithmetic on example numbers. Tariff = 25% × $20 import value = $5. Price rise = new price ÷ old price − 1. This page quotes no official tariff rate; for real rates, see US tariff rates by country and how to find your HTS code.
