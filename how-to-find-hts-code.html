How to find your product's HTS code and tariff rate

Your product's HTS code sets its ordinary US duty, and the country it comes from adds more on top. Seven steps take you from a product description to a total rate, with two worked examples checked at the official schedule on 25 November 2026: a cotton T-shirt from Vietnam pays 29%, a laptop from China nothing.

Seven steps from product to rate

  1. Describe the product. Write down what it is, what it is made of and in what share, how it is made (knitted or woven, for example) and what it is used for. The code follows from these facts, not from the product's name.
  2. Get a starting code from your supplier. Ask for the 6-digit HS code on the supplier's export papers. The first six digits are shared by most countries; the US adds two digits for the duty rate and two for statistics. Treat the supplier's code as a lead, not an answer.
  3. Search the Harmonized Tariff Schedule. Search hts.usitc.gov by keyword or by the 6-digit code. Open the chapter, read its notes for what it excludes, and work down the indented lines to the 8- or 10-digit line that matches your product's facts.
  4. Read the General rate. The General column of that line is the ordinary duty: a percentage, an amount per unit or kilo, or Free. The Special column lists lower rates under trade agreements, which you can claim only if the goods meet that agreement's origin rules.
  5. Add the duties for your origin. Look up the country-wide Section 301 duty for the country of origin, and for China the older Section 301 lists. If the product is steel, aluminum, copper, auto parts or certain wood products, the Section 232 duty applies instead of the country-wide duty.
  6. Check for exemptions. Some codes are exempt from the country-wide duty, such as laptops, phones and monitors. The exemptions are in note 52 of chapter 99, subchapter III, of the schedule.
  7. Confirm before you order. Search Customs rulings for the same product at rulings.cbp.gov, ask a licensed customs broker, or ask Customs for a binding ruling. The importer is responsible for the code, and a wrong one can mean back duty and penalties.

How the code is built

DigitsExampleWhat they say
2: chapter61Clothing, knitted or crocheted
4: heading6109T-shirts, singlets, tank tops
6: subheading (international HS)6109.10Of cotton
8: US rate line6109.10.00Where the US duty rate is set: 16.5%
10: statistical suffix6109.10.00.xxSplits the line for trade statistics; the rate does not change

Two products that look alike can sit under different lines because of one fact: knitted or woven, cotton or synthetic, with or without a lining. That is why step 1 matters most.

Worked example 1: cotton T-shirts from Vietnam

  1. Knitted T-shirt, 100% cotton, for adults.
  2. Chapter 61 (knitted clothing), heading 6109, subheading 6109.10 (of cotton), line 6109.10.00.
  3. General rate: 16.5%.
  4. Vietnam: +12.5% since 24 July 2026 (heading 9903.05.84), on top.
  5. T-shirts are not in the exemptions.
  6. Total: 29% of the customs value. A shirt bought for $4 pays $1.16.

Worked example 2: laptops from China

  1. Portable computer, under 10 kg, with keyboard and screen.
  2. Line 8471.30.01.
  3. General rate: Free.
  4. China's country-wide 12.5% would apply, and the older China lists could add more.
  5. But laptops are exempt from the 12.5% (note 52(b)), and their only older China list, 4B, is suspended.
  6. Total: 0% added duty today.

Same country, other product, a different answer. A country's headline rate tells you little until you know the code. More in tariff on electronics from China.

Mistakes that cost money

  • Trusting the supplier's code as final. Export codes are often chosen for the seller's convenience, and only the first six digits are shared.
  • Stopping at the General column. The added duties in chapter 99 are often larger than the ordinary duty.
  • Using a rate from before 24 July 2026. US rates changed three times in 2026. See US tariff rates by country.
  • Mixing up origin and shipping country. The duty follows where the goods were made, not where they were shipped from.

Questions

What is an HTS code?

The 10-digit code under which a product enters the United States, from the Harmonized Tariff Schedule. The first six digits are the international HS code; the next two set the US duty rate, and the last two are for statistics.

Where can I look up an HTS code for free?

On the US International Trade Commission's Harmonized Tariff Schedule site, hts.usitc.gov. Customs rulings on similar products are free to search at rulings.cbp.gov.

Is the HTS rate the whole tariff?

No. The General rate is the ordinary duty. Since 24 July 2026 most goods also pay a country-wide duty for their origin, and some pay Section 232 or older China Section 301 duties. A cotton T-shirt from Vietnam pays 16.5% plus 12.5%, so 29%, checked 25 November 2026.

Who decides the right HTS code?

The importer of record is responsible for it. Customs can disagree and charge the difference. For certainty before you import, ask U.S. Customs and Border Protection for a binding ruling.
