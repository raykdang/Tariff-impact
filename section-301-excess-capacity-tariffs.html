Section 301 excess capacity tariffs: the 16 economies, and what is official so far

The Section 301 excess capacity tariffs are not in force yet. On 25 November 2026 the U.S. Trade Representative made determinations for 14 of the 16 economies under investigation since 11 March 2026, and proposed tariffs on them, for public comment. No start date is set, and there is no determination yet for Mexico or Norway. Below: each economy's proposed rate, what its goods pay today, and how to test a rate on your own margin.

What the investigation is

On 11 March 2026 USTR opened investigations under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 into the "acts, policies, and practices of certain economies relating to structural excess capacity and production in manufacturing sectors." The notice names policies such as subsidies, wage suppression, state-owned enterprises and subsidized lending. It asked for comments on possible "tariff and non-tariff actions", but proposed no rate.

  • Opened: 11 March 2026; notice published in the Federal Register on 17 March 2026 (91 FR 12886, document 2026-05214); dockets USTR-2026-0067 for comments, USTR-2026-0068 for hearing requests.
  • Comments were due: 15 April 2026.
  • Public hearings: from 5 May 2026, continuing as needed to 8 May.
  • Determinations and proposed tariffs: 25 November 2026, for 14 economies; none yet for Mexico or Norway. Not in force, no start date.
  • Comments and hearing requests due: 5 January 2027. Hearings: from 25 January 2027.

Section 301 is the law USTR has leaned on since the Supreme Court ruled, on 20 February 2026 in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump, that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the President to impose tariffs. The country-wide duties in force today come from a different Section 301 action, described below.

Test a new rate on your margin now

You don't need to wait for the number to see what a rise would do. Take a product from China: supplier price $6.00, freight $0.60 a unit, sold at $15.00. Today's added duty is 12.5%. The second line adds 10 more points. That 10 points is a made-up example, not a forecast. USTR's proposal for China is up to 20%, net of today's duty; how the two combine is set in its notice, and nothing is in force.

What importers can do before a final decision

  • List your exposure. Note which of your products come from the 16 economies, and their tariff codes. That is the list you will check against the notice.
  • Watch for the date that counts. Earlier Section 301 actions applied to goods entered from a set hour, with a short grace period for goods already shipped. Read the final notice for both.
  • Work out your break-even price at a few rates; see absorb the tariff or raise prices and the tariff surcharge calculator.
  • Compare sources. Not every supplier country is among the 16; see China vs Vietnam vs Mexico vs India: landed cost compared.
  • Prepare your customers. The price increase letter templates are a start; send nothing until a rate is final.

Questions

What are the Section 301 excess capacity tariffs?

They are tariffs that may follow USTR's Section 301 investigations, opened on 11 March 2026, into excess manufacturing capacity in 16 economies. On 25 November 2026 USTR made determinations for 14 of them and proposed tariffs, for public comment. The proposed rates are not in force, and no start date is set.

Which countries are in the excess capacity investigation?

China, the European Union, Singapore, Switzerland, Norway, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, South Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Mexico, Japan and India.

Are the new tariffs on China and the EU in force?

No. As of 25 November 2026 the excess capacity tariffs are only proposed, with comments due 5 January 2027 and no start date. What is in force is the Section 301 forced-labor duty of 24 July 2026: 12.5% added on goods of China, and for the EU a duty that tops the ordinary duty up to 10%.

Will the new tariffs stack on the 12.5% and 10% duties?

USTR states each proposed rate net of duties already paid: for China, net of the 2026 forced-labor duty; for the others, net of existing Section 301 duties (and, for the EU, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland and Taiwan, of the ordinary duty too). How each combines with today's duty is set in that economy's notice. Nothing is final; plan for a few cases in the calculator.
