The formula, in words

  1. Tariff per unit = imported cost per unit × tariff rate.
  2. Surcharge per unit = tariff per unit × the share you pass on.
  3. Surcharge percentage = surcharge per unit ÷ selling price.

Two things trip people up.

The surcharge percentage is smaller than the tariff rate. The tariff is charged on what the goods cost you at import, not on what you sell them for. If you put the tariff rate itself on your invoice, you charge your customer far more than the tariff cost you.

Only count the new part. If you already paid some duty before and built it into your prices, the surcharge should cover the increase only: use the new rate minus the old rate. And if only part of your product is imported, use the cost of that part, not the whole unit.

If you sell under a contract or a purchase order with agreed prices, read it before you add a line. A surcharge the contract doesn't allow is a dispute waiting to happen.

Wording for the invoice or quote

Keep it short, name the cost, and don't call it a government charge on the customer. It is your cost, which you are passing on.

  • Line item: "Tariff surcharge (4.5% of goods): $9.00"
  • Note on a quote: "Prices include a tariff surcharge of 4.5%, shown separately. It covers import duties we pay on this product. We will reduce or remove it if those duties fall."
  • Note on an order form: "A tariff surcharge applies to imported items and is listed on its own line. It is reviewed whenever duty rates change."

Only promise a review if you will do one. For a full notice to customers, see the tariff price increase letter templates.

Sales tax can apply to the surcharge

A surcharge is usually part of what you charge for the goods, so states that tax the sale tend to tax the surcharge with it. Two states say so in plain words:

  • Washington: the Department of Revenue says surcharges, including tariffs, "are included in the selling price and are taxed", and that they can't be deducted "even if they're listed separately on the invoice".
  • New Jersey: the Division of Taxation says that when a seller passes the cost of a tariff to the purchaser, the charge is subject to sales tax as part of the taxable sales price, "even if the tariff is separately stated".

Other states have their own rules. Look up "tariff" or "surcharge" on your state revenue department's site, or ask your accountant, before you send the first invoice.

Where to find the rate you pay

Use the rate on your own paperwork, not a headline. Your customs broker's entry summary shows the duty paid on each shipment. For a product you haven't imported yet, look up its code in the official Harmonized Tariff Schedule and ask a broker which extra duties sit on top. U.S. duties changed several times in 2026, so check the date on anything you read, this page included.

Questions

How do I calculate a tariff surcharge?

Multiply the imported cost of the item by the tariff rate you pay, then by the share of the tariff you want the customer to cover. That is the surcharge in dollars. Divide it by your selling price to get the surcharge as a percentage of the price.

Is a tariff surcharge the same percentage as the tariff?

No. The tariff is charged on the imported cost of the goods, not on your selling price. A 25% tariff on an item that costs you $10 and sells for $30 is $2.50, which is 8.3% of the price.

Is sales tax charged on a tariff surcharge?

It depends on the state. Washington and New Jersey both say a tariff charge passed to the customer is part of the taxable selling price, even when it is listed on its own line. Check your own state's revenue department.

Should I add a markup to the surcharge?

That is your decision. A surcharge equal to the tariff keeps your profit in dollars the same. Some sellers add a little for the cash tied up in paying duty before the goods sell. If you do, don't describe the line as the tariff itself.
