China vs Vietnam vs Mexico vs India: landed cost compared for the same product

No country is cheapest for every product. A lower tariff doesn't mean a lower cost at your door: the supplier's price, the freight and the cost of moving all sit beside the duty. This page puts the four countries in one table, shows how to do the same for your product, and how to tell whether the gap is worth a move.

The sum

Landed cost per unit = supplier price + (supplier price × total duty rate) + freight and insurance per unit + entry fees per unit.

"Total duty rate" is the part people get wrong. For goods entering the United States it is usually a stack: the ordinary rate for the product's code in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, plus any additional duties that apply to that product, that country, or both. Two products from the same country can carry very different totals, and the same product can be covered by a trade agreement from one country and not from another.

Three things to notice.

  • The lowest duty rate did not produce a runaway winner. In this example the country with a 5% rate beats the one with a 30% rate by 57 cents a unit, about 4%, because its supplier price is higher.
  • A ten-point tariff gap vanished. The second column has a rate ten points lower than the first and still lands 6 cents higher, because the supplier charges 80 cents more.
  • Small changes reorder the table. Move any one supplier price by 5% and the ranking changes. That is the normal case, not a quirk of the example.

When the gap is too small to justify moving

Switching supplier has a one-off cost: samples, tooling or moulds, a factory visit or an inspection, new packaging and labels, your own time. Divide that cost by the saving per unit.

In the example, moving from the first country to the third saves $0.57 a unit. If the move costs $6,000, you need to buy about 10,500 units before it has paid for itself. At 500 units a month that is 21 months. U.S. tariffs changed several times within 2026 alone, so a payback that long is a bet on rates standing still.

A rule that holds up: move for a tariff gap only if it pays back inside the time you are confident about, and you would still be content with the new supplier if the gap closed.

What the table hides

  • Origin rules. The duty follows where the product is treated as made, not where it shipped from. Goods finished in one country from another country's parts may still count as the first country's product. Trade-agreement rates, such as those under the agreement between the United States, Mexico and Canada (USMCA), apply only to goods that meet that agreement's origin rules, with paperwork to prove it. Ask a customs broker before you rely on it.
  • Quality and defect rates. A supplier that is 4% cheaper and ships 6% more rejects is dearer.
  • Lead time. Longer transit means more stock and more cash tied up. Shorter transit can let you hold less.
  • Minimum order. A new supplier may want a larger first order than you can sell quickly.
  • Extra duties on particular goods. Some products carry anti-dumping or countervailing duties from particular countries. These can be far larger than the ordinary tariff.
  • Entry fees. See the next section.

What official sources say today, for these four countries

We don't publish a rate we haven't read at its source. On 25 November 2026 we read the following, and no more than this:

  • Additional duties under Section 301, for goods entered on or after 24 July 2026. A notice from the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative sets an added duty by country on products of many economies. For the four on this page it is 12.5% for China, 12.5% for Vietnam, 10% for India and 10% for Mexico.
  • Mexico's 10% has an exception. It does not apply to goods of Mexico that enter free of duty under USMCA.
  • Some goods are outside this layer. Goods covered by Section 232 duties, among them steel, aluminum, copper, vehicles and parts, and wood products, pay their own Section 232 rate and not this one. Laptops (8471.30.01), mobile phones (8517.13.00) and flat-panel monitors (8528.52.00) are exempt from it too: they pay only their ordinary duty under this layer (Harmonized Tariff Schedule, chapter 99, US note 52(b), heading 9903.05.86, read 25 November 2026). Toys and furniture are not on that list. We did not read the rest of the exempt lists.
  • This is one layer of the stack, not the total. The ordinary duty for your product's tariff code comes on top. For China, older Section 301 duties on listed products apply as well. We read them for a few goods on 25 November 2026 in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule (chapter 99, US note 20): wooden and plastic furniture, upholstered wooden seats included, are on list 3, +25%, with a few narrow exclusions until 9 January 2027; toys, laptops and monitors are only on list 4B, which is suspended, so none today; mobile phones are on no list. The 10% duty on Chinese goods of November 2025 (heading 9903.01.24) ended with the other emergency duties on 20 February 2026 (Executive Order 14389, 91 FR 9437) and does not come on top.
  • Merchandise Processing Fee. For formal entries the fee is 0.3464% of the goods' value, with a minimum of $34.58 and a maximum of $670.86 per entry, from 1 October 2026. On a small shipment the minimum matters more than the percentage: $34.58 on 200 units is 17 cents each.

This page quotes no ordinary duty rate, because that depends on your product's code. Look up your product at hts.usitc.gov, read the footnotes on its line for additional duties, and confirm the total with a customs broker.

How to rerun it for your product

  1. Get like-for-like quotes. Same specification, same quantity, same shipping terms. A quote that includes delivery and duty is not comparable with one that stops at the factory gate.
  2. Find the product's tariff code in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule, and the ordinary rate on that line.
  3. Find the additional duties for each origin. The footnotes on the tariff line point to them. A broker can confirm the total in a few minutes.
  4. Add freight, insurance and entry fees per unit for each route.
  5. Put the four totals in the calculator. Enter each country's unit cost, rate and shipping in turn, with your own selling price and volume, and compare total cost per unit, margin and monthly profit.
  6. Work out the payback on the cost of switching, as above.

Questions

Which country is cheapest to source from after tariffs?

There is no single answer, because the duty depends on the product's tariff code as well as the country, and the supplier's price and freight often matter more than the duty gap. Compare landed cost per unit for your own product: supplier price, plus duty, plus freight and fees.

How do I compare landed cost between two countries?

Get a quote for the same product and quantity from each country, find the total duty rate for the product's tariff code from each origin, and add freight, insurance and entry fees per unit. Landed cost per unit is the supplier price, plus the price times the duty rate, plus freight and fees.

When is a tariff gap big enough to switch supplier?

Divide the one-off cost of switching by the saving per unit. That gives the number of units you must buy before the move pays for itself. If that is more than you expect to buy before rates could change again, the gap is too small.
