Tariff price increase letter: three templates, with the number worked out

Three short letters you can copy today: one for wholesale and business customers, one for retail customers, and a notice for a shop counter or website. Below them: how to work out the percentage that goes in the gap.

Last reviewed: 25 November 2026. Templates and estimates only, not legal advice.

Letter 1: wholesale and business customers

Subject: Price change from [date]

Dear [name],

From [date], our prices on [product or range] will rise by [X]%. A price list with the new figures is attached.

The reason is the import duty we now pay on these goods, which has added [$ amount or %] to our cost per unit. We have [taken part of that ourselves / changed our shipping / held the rise to the affected lines only], and this change covers the rest. Products that are not affected stay at their current prices.

Orders placed before [date] will be invoiced at today's prices. [If you have a contract with us, its price terms continue to apply.]

We review these prices whenever duty rates change. If you would like to talk through your orders for the coming months, please reply to this email or call [name] on [phone].

Thank you for your business.

[Your name]
[Business]

Fill every square bracket, and keep only the sentences that are true for you.

Letter 2: retail customers (email or newsletter)

Subject: A small price change on [product or range]

Hi [name],

From [date], prices on [product or range] will go up by about [X]%. For example, [product] will be [$ new price] instead of [$ old price].

We import these products, and the duty we pay on them has gone up. We have covered part of that cost ourselves and kept the rise as small as we can. Everything else in the shop stays the same price.

Any order placed before [date] is at the current price.

Thanks for shopping with us. If you have a question, just reply to this email.

[Your name]
[Business]

Leave out "we have covered part of that cost ourselves" unless you have.

Letter 3: a notice for the counter, the menu or the website

A note on our prices

From [date], some of our prices are a little higher. The import duty on [goods, such as "the coffee we buy" or "our imported parts"] has gone up, and we can't cover all of it ourselves. We have kept the change as small as we can, and we will look at it again whenever those duties change. Thank you for your support.

The number that goes in the gap

A letter that says "about 4%" and can show why lands better than one that says "due to rising costs". The rise you quote should come from the tariff you pay, not from the tariff rate in the news. The two are different: the tariff is charged on what the goods cost you at import, and your customer pays a price that also covers freight, rent, wages and profit.

  1. Tariff per unit = imported cost per unit × the tariff rate you pay. Use the increase only if you already paid some duty before.
  2. Rise in dollars = tariff per unit × the share you are passing on.
  3. Rise as a percentage = rise in dollars ÷ your current price.

An example with round numbers, not today's rate for any product: an item costs you $12 to import and sells for $48. A tariff of 20% on $12 is $2.40. Passing all of it on is a rise of $2.40, which is 5% of the price. Passing on half is $1.20, or 2.5%.

How to state the rise honestly

  • Lead with the change, then the reason. What, how much, from when, why. Customers look for the number first.
  • Quote your figure, not the headline rate. "Our cost per unit rose by $2.40" is true and checkable. "Because of the 20% tariff" invites the customer to wonder why the price didn't rise 20%, or why it rose at all on goods that weren't affected.
  • Name the tariff only where it is the cause. If freight or wages are part of the rise, say "import duties and other costs". Blaming a tariff for a rise it didn't cause is a claim a customer or a regulator can test.
  • Give notice. Read your contracts and purchase orders first; some fix prices or set a notice period. Where nothing is fixed, a clear date and a promise to honour open orders does most of the work.
  • Say what you did. If you took part of the cost, changed supplier or cut another cost, one sentence on it earns more goodwill than a paragraph of apology.
  • One version for everyone in the same group. Different letters to similar customers get compared.

What not to promise about rolling it back

U.S. duty rates moved more than once in 2026 alone. A temporary 10% import surcharge ran from 24 February 2026 to 24 July 2026, and a different set of additional duties on products of many countries took effect on that same 24 July. Nobody can tell you where rates will be in six months, so don't tell your customers either.

  • Don't promise a date when the price will come down, or that it will fall by the same amount. Stock you bought at the higher cost still has to be sold before your own cost comes down.
  • Do promise something you control: "We review these prices whenever duty rates change."
  • Consider a surcharge line instead if you expect the tariff to be short-lived. It is easier to remove one line than to reprice a catalogue. See surcharge line or price rise.

Questions

How do I tell customers about a price increase due to tariffs?

Say what is changing, by how much, from which date and why, in that order and in a few sentences. Give notice before the change, say what you did to keep the rise small, and tell customers who to contact with questions.

How much notice should I give for a tariff price increase?

Check your contracts first, since some set a notice period. Where none applies, a common choice is to give trade customers around 30 days and honour orders already placed at the old price.

Should I promise to lower prices if the tariff is removed?

Only promise what you control. You can promise to review prices when duty rates change. Don't promise a date or an amount, because stock already bought at the higher cost still has to be sold.
