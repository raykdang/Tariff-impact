Understanding tariffs as a small business owner

If you import goods, tariffs are one of the biggest variables hitting your unit economics — and one of the most poorly understood. This guide explains what tariffs are, how they're calculated, and how to think about them strategically when you're running a small business with thin margins and limited pricing power.

What is a tariff?

A tariff is a tax that a government charges on imported goods. It's typically expressed as a percentage of the customs value (what you paid your supplier). When your shipment clears customs, you pay the tariff before you can take delivery. There's no avoiding it for products in scope — it lands directly in your cost of goods sold.

Tariffs come in three main flavors. The Most Favored Nation (MFN) rate is the baseline duty for a given Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS) code. Special program rates reduce or eliminate that baseline if your goods qualify under an FTA like USMCA, KORUS, or CPTPP. Add-on tariffs (like Section 301 on Chinese goods or Section 232 on steel and aluminum) layer on top of the MFN rate for political or strategic reasons.

How tariffs affect a small business specifically

Large retailers can absorb tariff cost increases by squeezing suppliers, hedging through scale, or accepting margin compression across hundreds of SKUs. Small businesses usually can't. Here's how the impact typically lands:

  • Margin compression hits faster. A 25% tariff on a product with 40% gross margin can wipe out 6-10 margin points overnight.
  • Pricing power is limited. If you sell on Amazon, eBay, or against bigger competitors, raising prices to recover tariff costs often loses more revenue than you gain.
  • Cash flow tightens. Tariffs are paid at customs clearance — weeks or months before you sell the goods. That ties up working capital you might not have.
  • Supplier switching is hard. Moving production to another country to cut the tariff sounds simple, and the gain can be smaller than it looks: since 24 July 2026, China and Vietnam carry the same 12.5% country-wide US duty, before China's older Section 301 duties. In practice, qualifying a new supplier takes 6-18 months, and minimum order quantities lock you in.

Example: e-commerce seller importing from China

You import housewares at $10/unit. With a 25% tariff (an example rate; check your own code) and $1.50/unit shipping, your landed cost is $14. You sell at $25, so margin is $11/unit. If a Section 301 add-on raises the effective tariff to 50%, your landed cost jumps to $16.50 and margin drops to $8.50 — a 23% hit. Spread across 1,000 monthly units, that's $2,500/month in lost profit.

Example: apparel boutique sourcing from Bangladesh

Apparel HTS codes carry some of the highest ordinary US duties (read 2026-11-25 in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule: cotton T-shirts 16.5%, cotton trousers 16.6%, woven synthetic jackets 27.7%), and since 24 July 2026 goods from Bangladesh pay 10% on top. At an example total of 25%, a boutique importing $20 dresses pays $5 in tariff per unit, before shipping. If demand is moderately price-elastic, even a small add-on can break the unit economics. Many small apparel sellers have shifted toward Vietnam, Cambodia, and Indonesia in response.

Strategic options when tariffs change

Most small business owners face the same set of levers when tariffs go up:

  1. Absorb the cost. Lower margin to keep prices steady. Works if competitors are also affected and you have the cash flow to take the hit short-term.
  2. Pass through to customers. Raise prices by some or all of the tariff increase. Works for price-insensitive products (specialty, branded, niche) but loses volume in commodity categories.
  3. Reformulate the product. Reduce unit cost by simplifying packaging, downsizing the product, or substituting components.
  4. Switch suppliers. Move sourcing to a country with lower or no tariff exposure. Usually the highest-effort option but largest long-term payoff.
  5. Apply for an exclusion. Section 301 has had product-specific exclusion programs at various points. Check the USTR website for active exclusion windows.
  6. Use a Foreign Trade Zone or duty drawback. If you re-export some inventory, you can recover duties paid. Also worth exploring: bonded warehouses to defer payment.

Frequently asked questions about tariffs

What is a tariff in simple terms?
A tariff is a tax that a government charges on goods imported from another country. It's typically a percentage of the import's customs value. So if your goods are worth $10,000 and the tariff rate is 10%, you pay $1,000 to customs before the goods can clear the border.

Who actually pays the tariff?
Legally, the importer of record pays the tariff at the port of entry — that's you, the small business. Economically, the cost gets distributed across the supply chain over time: some absorbed by importers, some passed to consumers, some squeezed back to suppliers. Most empirical studies of recent US tariffs find that 80-100% of tariff cost is ultimately passed to consumers within 6-12 months.

How do I find the exact tariff rate for my product?
Look up your product's HTS code (Harmonized Tariff Schedule) at hts.usitc.gov. The HTS code is product-specific (10 digits in the US) and determines your duty rate. Then check whether any special programs (USMCA, GSP, KORUS) or add-on tariffs (Section 301, Section 232) apply to your country of origin.

Should I absorb the tariff or raise prices?
It depends on your demand elasticity — how sensitive your customers are to price. For inelastic products (specialty goods, brand loyalty, no close substitutes), you can usually pass through most of the tariff with minimal volume loss. For elastic products (commodities, easy substitutes, price-sensitive customers), pass-through causes large demand drops and you may end up worse off than absorbing. What is Section 301?
Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 lets the U.S. impose extra tariffs on imports from countries the USTR finds engaging in unfair trade practices. The most-cited use is the Section 301 tariffs on Chinese goods, which have added 7.5% to over 100% on top of the MFN base rate, depending on the HTS code list. Section 301 lists are published in the Federal Register and updated periodically.

What is Section 232?
Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962 lets the U.S. impose tariffs on imports deemed to threaten national security. It's the basis for the steel and aluminum tariffs imposed on multiple countries in 2018 and renewed since. Section 232 hits steel-, aluminum-, and metal-product-heavy imports especially hard, including many goods from Turkey, Brazil, and India.

Are there ways to avoid or reduce tariffs legally?
Yes, several. (1) Source from a country with a free trade agreement: USMCA for Mexico/Canada, KORUS for Korea, etc. (2) Apply for an exclusion when programs are open. (3) Use Foreign Trade Zones (FTZs) or bonded warehouses to defer or reduce duties on re-exported goods. (4) Restructure the product so it qualifies under a different HTS code with a lower rate (this is legal classification, not fraud — be careful and consult a customs broker). (5) Use tariff engineering: small product modifications that move you to a lower-rate HTS code.

Can a small business apply for a tariff exclusion?
Yes, when exclusion windows are open. The USTR has run exclusion processes for Section 301 tariffs at various points. You file a petition arguing your product can't be sourced elsewhere, the tariff causes severe economic harm, or the product isn't strategically important to the policy goal. Approval rates have varied widely. Check ustr.gov for current exclusion windows.

How often do tariff rates change?
Base MFN rates change rarely (multi-year cycles). Add-on tariffs (Section 301, 232, 201) can change quickly via executive action — sometimes within days. FTAs can shift via renegotiation. For US imports specifically, tariff policy has been highly volatile in recent years.
