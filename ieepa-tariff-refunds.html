IEEPA tariff refunds: what ended in February 2026, and what still applies

The US emergency tariffs, imposed in 2025 under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), stopped on 24 February 2026. That includes the 10% worldwide "reciprocal" duty and its country rates, the fentanyl duties on Canada, Mexico and China, and Brazil's 40%. Customs (CBP) refunds IEEPA duty already paid, with interest, through a process called CAPE. Section 232 and Section 301 duties were not touched and still apply.

What happened, in three dates

  • 20 February 2026: the Supreme Court rules. In Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump (No. 24-1287, with Trump v. V.O.S. Selections, No. 25-250), the Court held: “IEEPA does not authorize the President to impose tariffs.”
  • 20 February 2026: Executive Order 14389, “Ending Certain Tariff Actions”. The same day, the President ordered that the IEEPA duties “shall no longer be in effect and, as soon as practicable, shall no longer be collected.”
  • 24 February 2026: Customs stops collecting. CBP’s message of 22 February 2026 (CSMS 67834313) says the duties “will no longer be collected for goods entered for consumption or withdrawn from warehouse for consumption, on or after 12:00 a.m. eastern time on February 24, 2026,” and that the IEEPA tariff numbers would be inactive in ACE from that day.

The national emergencies behind the duties “remain in effect”, the order says. So a later notice that an emergency “continues”, such as the one for Brazil in July 2026, does not bring the duty back.

Which tariffs ended

Executive Order 14389 names nine earlier orders. The additional IEEPA duties in each of them ended:

CBP’s message of 22 February 2026 names the first seven of these orders. The tariff schedule still prints some of the old lines, such as 9903.01.25 (the 10%) and 9903.01.77 (Brazil’s 40%), with no end note. The order lets the agencies tidy the schedule “by Federal Register notice”; until they do, the printed line is not a sign that the duty is collected.

What still applies

Executive Order 14389 says: “This order does not affect any other duties, including duties imposed under section 232 … and section 301.” It also leaves in place the order of the same day continuing the suspension of duty-free de minimis treatment for all countries. So on goods entered today:

  • The ordinary duty for your product’s code. See how to find your HTS code.
  • Section 232 duties on steel, aluminum, copper, cars and car parts, and some wood products. See what is Section 232.
  • The older Section 301 China duties, by list, and the country-wide Section 301 duties in force since 24 July 2026, including the separate 25% on most goods of Brazil. See US tariff rates by country.

Between the two regimes, a temporary 10% import surcharge under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 (Proclamation 11012 of 20 February 2026) applied to goods entered from 12:01 a.m. eastern time on 24 February 2026 through 12:01 a.m. eastern time on 24 July 2026, with exemptions that include goods under Section 232, duty-free USMCA goods of Canada and Mexico, energy, pharmaceuticals and certain electronics. It was a different law from IEEPA, and none of the refund messages we read covers it.

Getting IEEPA duty back: how CBP’s refund process works

CBP refunds IEEPA duties through CAPE, the “Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries”, a tab in the ACE Secure Data Portal. CBP says CAPE “is designed to consolidate refunds of IEEPA duties including interest rather than processing refunds on an entry-by-entry basis.” Here is what CBP’s own messages say, as read on 25 November 2026.

Who can file

The importer of record, or the authorized customs broker that filed the entries, with an established ACE Secure Data Portal account. The filer uploads a CSV file of up to 9,999 entry numbers; only the entry numbers are needed.

Which entries

  • Phase 1, opened 20 April 2026: “limited to certain unliquidated entries and certain entries within 80 days of liquidation.” An entry summary more than 80 days past its liquidation date is rejected.
  • Also rejected in that phase: type 09 reconciliation entries, drawback entries, type 08 USMCA duty-deferral entries, entries with an open or suspended protest, and anti-dumping or countervailing duty entries pending liquidation. Do not file entries on which a surety paid the IEEPA duties. File CAPE before any drawback claim.
  • From 29 June 2026: entry types 01, 02 and 06 flagged for reconciliation are accepted while no type 09 reconciliation entry has been filed (CSMS 69035485, 23 June 2026).
  • From 7 July 2026: warehouse entries (types 21 and 22) are rejected; file the warehouse withdrawals (types 31, 32, 34 and 38) instead (CSMS 69127837, 2 July 2026).

How and when the money comes

  • CBP removes the IEEPA chapter 99 numbers and recalculates the duty. Interest is calculated under 19 CFR 24.36.
  • Unliquidated entries are set to liquidate 45 days after the CAPE declaration is accepted; liquidated entries are reliquidated the next business day.
  • CBP says “valid IEEPA refunds will generally be issued within 60 - 90 days following acceptance of a CAPE Declaration.”
  • Refunds are consolidated by importer of record (or the party designated on CBP Form 4811) and liquidation date, paid “in one lump sum”, by ACH: “All refunds from CBP are issued electronically through ACH payments.” Bank details go in through the ACE Portal account.

What not to do

  • “Filers are prohibited from initiating an IEEPA duty refund request by filing a PSC” (a post-summary correction).
  • If you filed a protest only to get the IEEPA refund and the entry is within 80 days of liquidation, CBP says you may withdraw the protest and put the entry on a CAPE declaration. A suspended protest needs a request to the processing center.

CBP’s refund messages point to the court orders behind the process: the Court of International Trade’s order of 4 March 2026 in Atmus Filtration, Inc. v. United States (No. 26-01259), directing CBP to liquidate unliquidated entries “without regard to the IEEPA duties”, and its order of 7 April 2026 in Euro-Notions Florida, Inc. v. United States.

Your own entries are a question for a licensed customs broker. Which of your entries qualify, whether an entry outside Phase 1 can still be refunded, and what to do about protests depends on each entry’s status. CBP keeps adding to CAPE: check its “IEEPA Duty Refunds” page at cbp.gov before you file.

Example in dollars

A $10,000 entry in January 2026, a product whose ordinary duty is Free, that paid only the 10% worldwide duty (HTS 9903.01.25): IEEPA duty paid = $1,000. If the entry qualifies and the CAPE declaration is accepted, CBP refunds the $1,000 plus interest under 19 CFR 24.36, to the importer of record, by ACH. We have not worked out the interest: it depends on the dates.

If you bought DDP (your supplier or a courier was the importer of record), CBP pays the refund to them, not to you. Whether any of it reaches you is between you and them. See who pays the tariff under DDP, DAP and FOB.

Questions

Are the IEEPA tariffs still in effect?

No. Executive Order 14389 of 20 February 2026 ended the duties imposed under the emergency powers law (IEEPA), including the 2025 reciprocal duties and Brazil’s 40%. Customs stopped collecting them on goods entered on or after 12:00 a.m. eastern time on 24 February 2026. The national emergencies themselves stay in effect, but they no longer carry a duty.

Can I get a refund of the IEEPA tariffs I paid?

Customs (CBP) refunds IEEPA duties, with interest, through a process called CAPE in the ACE Secure Data Portal, opened on 20 April 2026. The importer of record or its authorized customs broker files a list of entry numbers. Phase 1 covers unliquidated entries and entries within 80 days of liquidation. Ask your customs broker which of your entries qualify.

How long does an IEEPA refund take?

CBP wrote on 13 April 2026 that valid IEEPA refunds will generally be issued within 60 to 90 days after a CAPE declaration is accepted. Unliquidated entries are set to liquidate 45 days after acceptance; liquidated entries are reliquidated the next business day. Refunds are paid by electronic transfer (ACH).

Which tariffs still apply after the IEEPA tariffs ended?

Executive Order 14389 says it does not affect any other duties, including Section 232 and Section 301 duties. So the ordinary duty, the Section 232 duties on steel, aluminum, cars and parts and wood, the older Section 301 China duties, and the country-wide Section 301 duties in force since 24 July 2026 all still apply.

Who receives the IEEPA refund?

CBP consolidates refunds by importer of record, or by the party designated to receive them on CBP Form 4811, and pays them by ACH. If a supplier, courier or marketplace was the importer of record on your goods, the refund goes to them, not to you.
