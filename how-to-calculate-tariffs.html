How to calculate tariffs on US imports, step by step

The duty on a shipment is customs value × duty rate. The duty rate is the ordinary rate for your product's code plus any added duties that apply to it, such as Section 301 or Section 232. Customs fees, the MPF and, for goods by ship, the HMF, come on top. This page explains each input, then works two shipments with every number shown.

The formula

LineSum
Duty rateOrdinary rate + Section 301 rate + Section 232 rate + any other added duty that applies
DutyCustoms value × duty rate
Merchandise Processing Fee (MPF)Customs value × 0.3464%, at least $34.58 and at most $670.86 per formal entry
Harbor Maintenance Fee (HMF)Customs value × 0.125%, only for goods unloaded from a ship at a US port
Total paid to CustomsDuty + MPF + HMF
Per unitTotal ÷ number of units

Most US duties are ad valorem: a percentage of the goods' value. Some codes charge an amount per unit or per kilo instead, or both; then the duty is quantity × that amount, and you add any percentage part on top. Your code's rate column shows which it is.

Each input, and where to find it

1. The HTS code

Every product has a ten-digit code in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States. The code sets the ordinary rate and decides which added duties reach the product. Get it from your supplier, your customs broker or a past entry, and check it yourself: see how to find your product's HTS code.

2. The customs value

Usually the price you actually paid or will pay the seller for the goods, in US dollars. International freight and insurance to the US are not part of it, so on a CIF or DDP price you take them out first. Some costs the seller doesn't bill, like moulds or materials you sent them, are added. The trade term in your quote decides what the price covers: see who pays the tariff under DDP, DAP and FOB.

3. The ordinary rate

The "General" column of the schedule for your code, unless you claim a trade agreement (the "Special" column) that your goods qualify for. Many products are Free; others run from a fraction of a percent to over 30%.

4. Added duties, and how they stack

  • Section 301, country-wide. Since 24 July 2026, most goods from a long list of countries pay an added 10% or 12.5% on top of the ordinary rate, with some countries topped up to a set total instead. See US tariff rates by country and Section 301 excess capacity tariffs.
  • Section 301, older China lists. Goods of China may also owe the duties from 2018 on, set by list. These add to the country-wide rate.
  • Section 232. Steel, aluminum, copper, vehicles and parts, some wood products and others pay their own Section 232 rate. Goods under Section 232 are exempt from the country-wide Section 301 rate, so you add one or the other, not both. See what is Section 232.
  • Anti-dumping and countervailing duties. Set product by product and often maker by maker. Check before you order: they can be larger than all the rest.

Rates add as percentage points: 16.5% ordinary plus 12.5% added is 29%, not 16.5% then 12.5% of the result. Each layer has exceptions, so read the layers for your own code and origin.

5. Customs fees

The MPF is charged on most formal entries; goods that qualify under some trade agreements, such as USMCA, are exempt. The HMF applies only to goods that arrive by ship. Neither is a tariff, and both can be owed on goods whose duty is Free: see tariff vs duty vs customs fees.

Example 1: cotton T-shirts from Vietnam, by ship

7,500 cotton T-shirts (HTS 6109.10.00) at $4 each, FOB, so a customs value of $30,000, as one formal entry. The ordinary rate, Vietnam's added rate and the HMF were read on 26 November 2026; the MPF was read on 25 November 2026. Sources are listed below.

The duty is 98% of what Customs collects here. A shirt that cost $4 now costs $5.18 once Customs is paid, before freight and the broker's fee. To see what that does to your margin and price, open this case in the calculator.

From duty to price

The duty is a cost on the goods' value, not on your selling price. A 29% duty on a $4 shirt sold at $18 adds $1.16, which is 6.4% of the price, not 29%. See does a 25% tariff raise prices by 25%? for the sum, and the break-even price after a tariff for the price that keeps your margin.

Questions

How do you calculate a tariff?

Multiply the customs value of the goods by the duty rate. The duty rate is the ordinary rate for the product's HTS code plus any added duties that apply, such as a Section 301 country rate or a Section 232 rate. Then add customs fees: the Merchandise Processing Fee on a formal entry, and the Harbor Maintenance Fee if the goods arrive by ship.

Is the tariff charged on the price I pay or the price I sell at?

On the customs value, which is usually the price you paid the seller for the goods, not counting international freight and insurance to the US. It is not charged on your selling price, so a 29% duty on a $4 item adds $1.16 whatever you sell it for.

Do Section 301 and Section 232 duties stack?

Section 301 and ordinary duties stack: you add the percentage points. The country-wide Section 301 duties in force since 24 July 2026 do not apply to goods that pay a Section 232 duty, so for steel, aluminum, copper, vehicles and parts or wood products you add the Section 232 rate instead. Older China Section 301 duties are set by list and can add to the country-wide rate.

What fees are added to the duty?

The Merchandise Processing Fee is 0.3464% of the value on a formal entry, at least $34.58 and at most $670.86, from 1 October 2026. The Harbor Maintenance Fee is 0.125% of the value of goods unloaded from a ship. A customs broker's fee and freight are further costs, but they are not paid to Customs.

Where do I find my product's duty rate?

In the Harmonized Tariff Schedule of the United States, at hts.usitc.gov, under your product's HTS code: the General column gives the ordinary rate. Added duties sit in chapter 99 of the same schedule, by country or by product.
